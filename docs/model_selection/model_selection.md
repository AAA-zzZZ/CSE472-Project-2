# Boosted hurdles rank risk; ensembles capture loss

The best primary model for this project is a **two-part (hurdle) LightGBM**: a binary occurrence model times a severity model for positive losses. On the project's own 2017-2023 test set it beats every alternative on discrimination: **ROC-AUC 0.886, PR-AUC 0.252, Brier skill 0.150, AUC within month 0.848, county-year rank correlation 0.303, and MAE on damaged months 0.816**. Its ROC-AUC gain over the GLM hurdle is **+0.026 (95% county-bootstrap interval +0.021 to +0.031)**. But it does not win the metric closest to emergency allocation, the share of loss captured by each month's top 5% of counties. There the GLM hurdle (0.736 on all months, helped by March 2019) and the random-forest hurdle (0.756 once the largest month is dropped) lead, and LightGBM trails in the median month (0.366 against 0.408-0.409). The recommended stack therefore has these parts:

- **Primary model:** a tuned LightGBM hurdle, with a rank-averaged ensemble with the GLM and RF hurdles kept as the allocation score.
- **Second method (instructor-required):** an R-INLA BYM2 spatio-temporal hurdle, ideally fed LightGBM predictions as covariates.
- **Causal estimate:** a county and time fixed-effects panel, then DML (FE-DML for the average effect, `CausalForestDML` for tau(w; x, c)).
- **Calibration:** a state-level intercept (Saerens/Platt) recalibration fitted on 2017-2018.
- **Intervals:** hurdle-structured conformal intervals updated with adaptive conformal inference.

The single-part LightGBM Tweedie, zero-inflated count models and deep learning should be dropped or kept only as footnotes. Two findings frame everything else. First, **skill depends on same-month observed hazard**: restricted to inputs known in advance, the LightGBM hurdle falls to ROC-AUC 0.769, only +0.041 above a county-by-month climatology. Second, **every model over-predicts the test damage rate by 1.28-1.43x**, because the reported damage rate fell from 3.54% to 2.52%. Literature on this exact problem is thin and was mostly read from abstracts, so the project's benchmark carries the most weight below.

## LightGBM wins discrimination on the project's own test set, but not top-5% loss capture

The project's benchmark (`model_comparison.py`, run 2026-11-01; first-hand evidence, `own_benchmark.md`) uses the same split as `hurdle_model.py`. Training covers 2000-2016 (215,220 county-months, 3.54% with damage) and the test covers 2017-2023, scored once (88,620 county-months, 2,234 with damage, 2.52%). Tree models use all 39 inputs plus state and calendar month. Their tree counts were chosen by early stopping on 2013-2016 and the models were then refit on 2000-2016. The GLM is the existing ridge logistic plus linear hurdle on 17 inputs.

| County x month rate | 0.728 | 0.066 | 0.000 | 1.403 | 0.709 | 0.211 | 0.266 | 0.023 | 0.113 | 0.905 |
| GLM hurdle | 0.859 | 0.193 | 0.114 | 1.372 | 0.824 | **0.736** | 0.742 | **0.408** | 0.259 | 0.862 |
| Random forest hurdle | 0.879 | 0.216 | 0.141 | 1.411 | 0.837 | 0.608 | **0.756** | **0.409** | 0.252 | 0.829 |
| **LightGBM hurdle** | **0.886** | **0.252** | **0.150** | 1.276 | **0.848** | 0.580 | 0.723 | 0.366 | **0.303** | **0.816** |

The occurrence results are clear. **LightGBM beats the GLM by +0.0263 ROC-AUC (+0.0213 to +0.0309)**, and RF beats the GLM by +0.0196 (+0.0143 to +0.0241), on 200 county-bootstrap samples. LightGBM's PR-AUC of 0.252 is 30% higher than the GLM's 0.193 and roughly 10x the 2.5% prevalence. It also leads on AUC within month, which removes the "which months are stormy" signal and tests whether the model picks the right counties inside a given month. That is the closest occurrence metric to the allocation task.

Loss capture tells a different story, and the tension is the most important design fact in this report. The all-months top-5% figure is loss-weighted, and **March 2019 alone holds 21% of test loss**. The GLM's 0.736 mostly reflects catching that one event. Without it, the order becomes RF 0.756 > GLM 0.742 > LightGBM 0.723. In the median month, GLM and RF tie at about 0.41 and LightGBM drops to 0.366. So LightGBM separates damaged from undamaged county-months best, but its expected-loss ranking (P times severity) concentrates dollars less well. Two explanations fit the numbers, and both can be tested. Either the severity stage, fitted on about 7,600 training positives, is too flexible, or the product of a better classifier and a noisier severity model reorders the top of the list. Three actions follow:

- **Tune the severity model separately**, with shallow trees, strong regularisation and monotone constraints on hazard inputs.
- **Score allocation with a rank average** of the LightGBM, RF and GLM expected-loss scores, a cheap ensemble the literature also favours ([Shwartz-Ziv & Armon](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.03253v2)).
- **Report top-k results per month with bootstrap intervals.** The between-model gaps on top-5% have no confidence intervals yet, and one month can swing them.

Two confounds in the benchmark need closing before the write-up. First, the GLM sees 17 inputs and the trees see 39 plus state and month. Part of the tree advantage may therefore come from inputs rather than the learner. Refitting the GLM on the same inputs, or LightGBM on the GLM's 17, would separate the two. Second, the top LightGBM input by gain is `p_month` (31.6% of gain). It is the county's total rainfall in that month from NOAA nClimGrid-Daily, an input built without SHELDUS, so it is not target leakage; its dominance is the same-month-hazard effect described below. (An earlier draft of this report misread it as a damage-rate prior.)

## The literature agrees on trees, splits on Tweedie, and rules out ZIP and deep nets

No published study benchmarks hurdle GBM, Tweedie GBM, RF and GLM hurdles on SHELDUS or any county-month flood-loss data. The project's own comparison is therefore both the strongest evidence and a contribution in its own right. Adjacent evidence agrees with it on the main point. Tree ensembles beat depth-damage functions and parametric models on flood-loss data: [Merz, Kreibich & Lall 2013](https://nhess.copernicus.org/articles/13/53/2013/), [Wagenaar et al. 2017](https://doaj.org/article/350ff8eae946441fb868c26390f37be0) and BT-FLEMO ([Kreibich et al. 2017](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wly:riskan:v:37:y:2017:i:4:p:774-787)). A two-stage random forest (damaged or not, then amount) reached about **81% occurrence accuracy on more than 14,000 Southeast US flash-flood events** [snippet] ([Alipour et al. 2020](https://moradkhani.ua.edu/archives/2393)). A CONUS random forest for flood-damage probability reached **AUC 0.75** [snippet] ([Collins et al. 2022, USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/predicting-flood-damage-probability-across-conterminous-united-states)). On NFIP claims, XGBoost beat GANs and Gaussian processes in high-claim counties [snippet] ([Salas et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.08660)). The project's 0.886 sits well above Collins's 0.75. Part of the reason is that it conditions on observed same-month hazard, as discussed in the next section.

**Tweedie.** Here the project's data overrule the literature-based hypothesis. Actuarial work shows boosted Tweedie beating Tweedie GLMs ([Yang, Qian & Zou](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/insurance-premium-prediction-via-gradient-tree-boosted-tweedie-co/)). It also shows zero-inflated Tweedie boosting beating plain Tweedie under heavy zero inflation ([So & Valdez 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.16206v1); [Zhou, Qian & Yang](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1811.10192)). The research notes had predicted that a Tweedie GBM would match a hurdle on loss capture. Instead, the project's LightGBM Tweedie (power 1.5, untuned, stopped at 120 trees) loses to the LightGBM hurdle by **0.0218 ROC-AUC (+0.0164 to +0.0266)**. It is not significantly better than the GLM (+0.0046, interval -0.0007 to +0.0098), and it has the **worst top-5% capture of any learned model (0.450)**. That matches the only direct hurdle-vs-Tweedie GBM comparison found, where the hurdle won and the fixed-power Tweedie had negative R² [snippet] ([MDPI Applied Sciences 16(13):6550](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/16/13/6550)). With 96.8% zeros, one variance power cannot serve both occurrence and a very heavy tail. A grid over power 1.1-1.9 would make the comparison fair. The current gap is too large to make Tweedie a contender, so it belongs in an appendix.

**Library choice.** The choice among LightGBM, XGBoost and CatBoost is second-order. In auto-claims comparisons, **CatBoost was the most accurate and LightGBM the fastest** [snippet] ([So 2023/24](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.07771)). Across 176 tabular datasets, CatBoost and TabPFN ranked best overall ([McElfresh et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.02997v4)). A CatBoost hurdle is a reasonable robustness row, but LightGBM's measured lead over RF and the GLM matters more than any library difference.

**Zero-inflated models.** ZIP and ZINB are count models. SHELDUS losses are continuous dollars, so these models only apply if the target becomes a count of damage events. R-INLA's built-in zero-inflated likelihoods also have an intercept-only zero probability ([R-INLA doc](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/grantbrown/inla/master/r-inla.org/doc/likelihood/zeroinflated.tex)). That is wrong for an outcome whose zero probability varies by county and season. The hurdle is the correct zero model.

**Deep learning.** DL is not worth the effort here. On medium-sized tabular data, tuned trees stay ahead and the gap does not close with tuning ([Grinsztajn et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.08815)). GBDTs are "much better" than neural nets on skewed, heavy-tailed features ([McElfresh et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.02997v4)). XGBoost beat four deep tabular models on 8 of 11 datasets ([Shwartz-Ziv & Armon](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.03253v2)). This project has about 300K rows but only about 9,700 positives, with hand-built, skewed inputs. Published LSTM and GNN flood models predict hazard (flooded tracts, inundation, water levels), not monetary loss ([arXiv 2111.08450](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.08450); [FloodGNN-GRU](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/environmental-data-science/article/floodgnngru-a-spatiotemporal-graph-neural-network-for-flood-prediction/93BA1DA8D6ECC93D985656C3BC1EA3DE)). Lagged and neighbour-county features inside the GBM capture the same sequence and spillover signal more cheaply.

## Same-month hazard carries most of the skill, so call the model an impact model

The "known in advance only" row is the benchmark's most consequential result for how the project describes itself. With only inputs available before the month starts, the LightGBM hurdle reaches **ROC-AUC 0.769 and PR-AUC 0.093**, against 0.886 and 0.252 with same-month hazard. The prior-only model's gain over a county-by-month climatology is real (+0.0409, interval +0.0327 to +0.0478), but its top-5% share in the median month is only 0.077. The input ranking points the same way. After the climatological prior, the top inputs are same-month hazard measures:

- the ratio of 5-day maximum rainfall to its return level (7.1% of gain);
- rain on wet soil (3.4%);
- snowmelt (3.2%);
- Rx5day (2.7%).

This matches the earlier driver review: wet antecedent soils, snowmelt and frozen ground produce Midwest floods ([Berghuijs et al. 2016](https://typeset.io/pdf/dominant-flood-generating-mechanisms-across-the-united-10tztxaz7l.pdf); [Iowa DNR on March 2019](https://iowadnr.gov/About-DNR/DNR-News-Releases/ArticleID/2384/Water-Summary-Update-Rapid-runoff-led-to-historic-flooding-in-March)).

The implication is a framing choice, not a modelling flaw. The primary model is a **conditional impact model**: it ranks counties for resources once the month's hazard is observed or forecast. Used that way, it can take a weather forecast in place of observations, as the 2026 XGBoost flash-flood study does with medium-range inputs [snippet] ([EGUsphere 2026-1591](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2026/egusphere-2026-1591/)). The project should report both rows and state which one supports which decision. Random splits overstate disaster-claims skill relative to temporal splits [snippet only] ([Frontiers in AI 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/artificial-intelligence/articles/10.3389/frai.2026.1802461/full)). The project's single, untouched 2017-2023 test is the right protection and should stay that way.

## Calibration must undo a 1.4x base-rate shift before amounts or intervals mean anything

Every model's mean predicted probability exceeds the test damage rate: **1.28x for LightGBM, 1.37x for the GLM, 1.41x for RF and 1.40x even for the climatology**. That the climatology is also off shows the cause is a drop in recorded damage between 2000-2016 and 2017-2023, not model error. Ranking metrics (AUC, PR-AUC, top-k) do not change under a monotone recalibration. Expected dollar amounts and interval coverage do, because they multiply P(loss>0) by severity.

The cheapest fix is the prior-shift correction of [Saerens, Latinne & Decaestecker (2002)](https://dipot.ulb.ac.be/dspace/bitstream/2013/68391/1/Decaestecker_NeuralComp02.pdf). It moves each logit by log-odds(new rate) minus log-odds(old rate), which equals a Platt recalibration with the slope fixed at 1. With the project's rates of 3.54% and 2.52%, the shift is about **-0.35 on the logit scale** (my calculation). Two refinements matter.

- **Fit by state.** The over-prediction reaches 2.5x in some states, so the correction should be a state-specific intercept.
- **Use a free slope as well.** Reporting declines plausibly remove mostly small losses. That breaks the label-shift assumption that P(x|y) stays fixed, which a fixed-slope shift cannot absorb. Platt with a free slope can.

Isotonic recalibration needs more than about 1,000 calibration samples and adds ties that can change AUC ([scikit-learn calibration guide](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scikit-learn/scikit-learn/main/doc/modules/calibration.rst)). It suits only the largest calibration windows. In scikit-learn, `CalibratedClassifierCV(FrozenEstimator(model))` recalibrates a fitted model on separate data ([same source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scikit-learn/scikit-learn/main/doc/modules/calibration.rst)).

The honest design problem is timing. A 2013-2016 calibration window cannot see a drop that begins in 2017, and using the 2017-2023 rate itself would be an oracle. The defensible protocol is to **calibrate on 2017-2018 and report calibrated metrics on 2019-2023**, which still contains March 2019. Uncalibrated full-period ranking metrics stay as they are, and a yearly rolling recalibration can be added as a sensitivity check. The tables should show mean P / rate by year and state, reliability diagrams, Brier and log loss, "frozen vs recalibrated" side by side, and the calibrated severity product with Duan smearing on any log-scale severity.

## A BYM2 hurdle in INLA is the right second method, strongest as a hybrid

For county polygons, **BYM2 with PC priors** is the standard spatial prior. It scales the ICAR component so that its mixing parameter reads as the spatially structured share of variance, and it "performs at least equally well" as older parameterisations ([Riebler et al. 2016](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/1601.01180)). SPDE models suit point-referenced data, not county aggregates. The hurdle should be a **joint INLA model with two stacked likelihoods**: a binomial on all 303,840 rows and a Gaussian on log amount for the positive rows. The occurrence part's BYM2 county effect is reused in the severity part through `copy=` with a free scaling coefficient. This construction appears in an inlabru course practical and in R-INLA discussion threads ([inlabru practical](https://inlabru-courses.github.io/NewCaledonia2026/practical_zero_inflated.html); [R-INLA BYM2 hurdle thread](https://groups.google.com/g/r-inla-discussion-group/c/3HWyQ78OtQA)). Sharing matters because the severity part has few rows per county and borrows strength from the data-rich occurrence part.

Time should enter as main effects: rw1 or ar1 for year, cyclic rw2 for calendar month, and if needed a county-by-year interaction (25,320 nodes). A full Knorr-Held Type IV county-by-month interaction is out of reach: it adds 303,840 latent nodes with 1,342 constraints, and that many constraints make such models "hard to compute" ([Aanes & Storvik](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.11851)). INLA's newer formulation is built for this case of low-dimensional latent structure with high-dimensional data ([van Niekerk et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.06797)). No timing benchmark at 300K binomial rows was found, though, so fitting time is an inference. If INLA stalls, brms `hurdle_lognormal` with `car(type="bym2")` is the MCMC fallback. It is untested at this scale, and its generated Stan code should be checked ([brms car docs](https://rdrr.io/pkg/brms/man/car.html); [Stan Discourse](https://discourse.mc-stan.org/t/considering-spatial-autocorrelation-in-hurdle-model-using-brms/25368)).

Expectations should be modest. In the closest flood application, a French insurance study, spatial effects improved claim *occurrence* clearly and *severity* only slightly [snippet, preprint] ([Moriah et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.07609)). In a comparison of INLA-SPDE with random forest, point predictions were similar and INLA's uncertainty was wider [snippet] ([arXiv 2609.20038](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.20038)). A covariate-only BYM2 hurdle will likely land near the project's GLM (ROC-AUC about 0.86), not above LightGBM. The stronger version is the published hybrid. In "XGBoost meets INLA", XGBoost forecasts enter an INLA latent Gaussian model as a synthetic covariate beside spatial and temporal effects, and the INLA model then produces the posterior predictive distribution ([Hu et al. 2026, Environmetrics](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.09896)). INLA-RF does the same with a random-forest offset [snippet] ([arXiv 2507.18488](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.18488v1)). For this project, that means adding out-of-fold (time-blocked) logit P and predicted log amount from LightGBM to the two INLA parts as covariates. The BYM2 map then shows residual spatial risk that the trees missed, and the model yields calibrated predictive distributions. Coefficients on spatially smooth inputs, such as rain climatology, will shift once BYM2 is added ([Hodges & Reich 2010](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/adding-spatially-correlated-errors-can-mess-up-the-fixed-effect-y/)). Restricted spatial regression gives poor coverage on areal data ([Khan & Calder](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1905.09371)). The project should therefore report those coefficients with and without the spatial term and make no causal claim from them.

## Causal tau(w; x, c) needs fixed effects first and DML second

The DAG's backdoor path W <- C -> A -> Y runs through rain climate and adaptation, both of which are fixed or slow-moving within a county. **County fixed effects absorb C and the time-invariant part of A.** That leaves within-county hazard deviations as good as random, the core identification argument of climate econometrics ([Hsiang 2016](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22181)). The nearest flood precedent, Davenport, Burke & Diffenbaugh, used a **state-by-month fixed-effects panel** on more than 6,600 damage reports. They attributed about **36% of 1988-2017 US flood damage (about $73B)** to historical precipitation change ([PNAS / PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/7848586/)). Time fixed effects (year-month, or state-by-year) bring a further benefit specific to this project: they absorb the post-2016 reporting decline. If the decline does not depend on W, it then biases levels but not the within-period effect of W. Recalibration fixes the predictive base rate; the fixed effects fix the causal one.

Machine learning enters in two layers:

- **Average effect, flexibly controlled.** FE-DML, through DoubleML's new `DoubleMLPLPR`, implements the Clarke & Polselli panel extension of DML. Its options are first-difference, within-group and correlated-random-effects ([DoubleML source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DoubleML/doubleml-for-py/main/doubleml/plm/plpr.py); [Clarke & Polselli](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.08174v5)). In their simulations, least squares does well even under nonlinearity, and DML's bias reduction pays off when the true effect is nonlinear or discontinuous. That makes the FE panel the baseline and DML the robustness check, not the reverse.
- **Heterogeneity.** EconML's `CausalForestDML` accepts a continuous treatment (`discrete_treatment=False`) and gives intervals by Bootstrap-of-Little-Bags ([EconML README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/py-why/EconML/main/README.md)). C and X go in as effect modifiers. Calendar and year controls go in the confounder slot, with the hazard county-demeaned to mimic the fixed effects. The DR-learner and DoubleML's APO models need a discrete treatment, so they apply only to binned hazard classes.

Three safeguards apply. **Check overlap**: wet-climate counties may never see the hazard percentiles that dry counties see. Expressing W as county-specific percentiles builds overlap in by design, and tau(w) should be restricted to the observed support of w within each C bin. **Allow crossing curves**: adapted counties can do better at moderate w and fail at extreme w, as found across 45 paired flood events ([Kreibich et al.](https://lup.lub.lu.se/record/a6a9b4e5-0910-4d1d-803f-0e353caea896)). **Cluster inference** by county and month, because one storm hits many neighbours at once. The hurdle structure also applies here. Estimate the effect on P(Y>0) and on log loss given Y>0 separately, or use log(1+Y). Lagged adaptation measures partly encode past floods, so A belongs among the controls, not among the reported effects. No published DML or causal-forest study of hazard intensity on county flood loss was found, so this estimate is new.

## Conformal intervals must follow the hurdle and adapt to the shift

With 96.8% zeros, an interval of {0} covers almost every county-month. Marginal coverage is therefore nearly meaningless, and coverage has to be reported **conditional on Y>0**, by year, by state and by risk decile. The structure that fits is the classification-powered conformal approach for zero-inflated outcomes. A classifier decides between {0} and a positive part, and conformal regression builds the interval for the positive part ([Li et al., arXiv 2605.04219](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.04219)). This is a 2026 preprint with no code or replication found, and a claimed 60-80% shortening of intervals is a secondary, unverified summary. For the severity part, **conformalized quantile regression (CQR)** on LightGBM quantile models ([Romano et al. 2019](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scikit-learn-contrib/MAPIE/master/README.md)) is supported directly by MAPIE's `ConformalizedQuantileRegressor` ([MAPIE source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scikit-learn-contrib/MAPIE/master/mapie/regression/quantile_regression.py)).

The base-rate shift breaks the exchangeability that split conformal assumes. Static intervals calibrated before 2017 will likely over-cover zeros and miss the shifted positives. Two methods handle this:

- **Adaptive conformal inference** updates the miscoverage level after each month (alpha_{t+1} = alpha_t + gamma(alpha - err_t)). It guarantees long-run coverage frequency whatever the data-generating process ([Gibbs & Candès 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.00170v3)) and is available as MAPIE `TimeSeriesRegressor(method="aci")` ([MAPIE source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scikit-learn-contrib/MAPIE/master/mapie/regression/time_series_regression.py)).
- **Weighted conformal**, which gives recent calibration months more weight ([Barber et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.13415)).

ACI was designed for one sequence, and counties in the same month are spatially correlated. The guarantee therefore holds over months, so run ACI on pooled monthly miscoverage or per state. The useful demonstration is static split conformal against ACI, with coverage and width at 80% and 90%, the Winkler interval score, and a rolling 12-month coverage curve. The INLA posterior predictive intervals provide a model-based comparison on the same table.

## Recommended stack and the order to build it

| Role | Choice | Why | Keep as comparison |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary predictive | LightGBM hurdle (binary occurrence + regularised log-severity, monotone +1 on hazard inputs, early stopping on 2013-2016) | Best AUC, PR-AUC, Brier skill, within-month AUC, rank correlation and MAE on the project's test | GLM hurdle, RF hurdle, county x month climatology |
| Allocation score | Rank average of LightGBM, RF and GLM expected loss | GLM and RF beat LightGBM on top-5% loss share (median month 0.41 vs 0.37) | Each member alone |
| Second method | R-INLA joint hurdle: binomial + Gaussian-on-log, shared BYM2 (`copy`), cyclic month, rw1 year; then the hybrid with out-of-fold LightGBM predictions as covariates | Instructor requirement; pooling for sparse counties; predictive distributions; published XGBoost+INLA template | iid county effect (ablation); with/without BYM2 coefficients |
| Causal | County + year-month FE panel with W x C interactions; FE-DML (`DoubleMLPLPR`); `CausalForestDML` for tau(w; x, c) | FE blocks W <- C -> A; time FE absorb reporting drift | Binned-W DR-learner |
| Calibration | State-intercept Platt/Saerens on 2017-2018, scored on 2019-2023 | Fixes the 1.28-1.43x over-prediction without changing the ranking | Isotonic; frozen |
| Intervals | Hurdle conformal: calibrated classifier -> {0} or CQR interval, updated by ACI | Zero-heavy structure; shift-robust long-run coverage | Static split conformal; INLA posterior intervals |
| Drop | Tweedie GBM (appendix only, after a power grid), ZIP/ZINB, MLP/LSTM/GNN | Tweedie loses on every metric; ZIP fits counts, not dollars; DL loses on tabular data | - |

The build order follows the dependencies:

1. **Close the benchmark's open questions** (about a day): rerun the GLM on matched inputs, and add month-block bootstrap intervals for top-k. Every later comparison rests on these numbers.
2. **Tune the LightGBM hurdle**: monotone constraints, a separate severity search, the ensemble allocation score, and an optional CatBoost row.
3. **Add calibration**, because amounts, Brier scores and intervals all depend on it.
4. **Build the INLA BYM2 hurdle and then the hybrid.** It is the instructor-required method and takes the longest to get running, so start it right after the primary model is stable rather than leaving it last.
5. **Run the causal analysis**: the fixed-effects panel first, then FE-DML, then the causal forest with overlap checks.
6. **Add conformal intervals**, built on the calibrated classifier and compared with the INLA intervals.

## Conclusion

The project's own test results shift the question from "which algorithm wins" to "which score serves which decision". A boosted hurdle is the best detector of which county-months will see damage. Yet a simpler GLM and a random forest put more dollars in the top 5% of counties, and one storm month can reverse the order. The two metrics measure different things, so the allocation score should be an ensemble judged per month with intervals, not the single best AUC model. A second shift matters just as much. Most measured skill comes from observing the month's hazard, so the honest claim is a strong impact model plus a modest advance-warning model, and the gap between them (0.886 against 0.769 AUC) is itself a finding worth reporting.

The reporting decline after 2016 links the predictive and causal halves of the project. In the predictive model it is a calibration problem, solved with a state-level intercept fitted on 2017-2018. In the causal model it is a time-varying measurement error that year fixed effects remove. One data artefact, handled two ways, gives the write-up a coherent account of why predicted levels need correcting while the estimated effect of hazard on loss holds.
