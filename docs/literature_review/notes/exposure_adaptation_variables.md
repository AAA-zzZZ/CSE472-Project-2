## Q1. Exposure variables: which are used and how much do they explain?

### Takeaway
County flood-loss studies consistently proxy exposure with housing units or population, and the best ones use **housing units located in the FEMA high-risk floodplain**. In one Indiana county panel, growth in floodplain housing accounted for most of projected damage growth (87%). Developed and impervious land (NLCD) and property value are the standard second-tier exposure measures. Structure-level inventories (USACE NSI, First Street) give floodplain counts and values, but they are recent snapshots, not 2000–2023 time series.

### Cited Findings
- Liu, Hertel & Diffenbaugh, "Future property damage from flooding: sensitivities to economy and climate change," *Climatic Change* (2015), DOI 10.1007/s10584-015-1478-z. Design: county panel of Indiana counties, 1995–2012, **SHELDUS** flood property damage (coastal flooding excluded), Tobit with county fixed effects. Exposure = county housing units in high flood-risk zones, built from FEMA floodplain maps and Census data. — [OSTI record](https://www.osti.gov/scitech/biblio/1265732-future-property-damage-from-flooding-sensitivities-economy-climate-change); [Stanford EMF presentation](https://web.stanford.edu/group/emf-research/docs/PIAMDDI/Liu%20et%20al_Flooding_PI_2013.pdf)
- Same study: more housing units (the exposure proxy) lead to more damage. Projected rise in annual Indiana flood damage is about $43M, "87% of which is attributed to more houses located in high risk flood zones." — [Stanford EMF presentation](https://web.stanford.edu/group/emf-research/docs/PIAMDDI/Liu%20et%20al_Flooding_PI_2013.pdf); [OSTI](https://www.osti.gov/pages/servlets/purl/1265732)
- Tellman et al. (2020, *Sustainability* 12(15):6006) normalized county SHELDUS flood property damage by **2010 housing value**, an exposure-normalization approach, across 11,629 non-coastal US flood events from 2008–2012, controlling for flood intensity from stream gauges. — [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/12/15/6006); [USU Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/envs_facpub/1612)
- Other SHELDUS work normalizes damages per capita at the **county-month** level (FRBSF working paper 2020-34). Some studies add a capital-stock proxy (income × population). — [FRBSF WP 2020-34](https://www.frbsf.org/wp-content/uploads/wp2020-34.pdf); [search summary of NY Fed SR1135](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr1135.pdf)
- Brody, Zahran, Highfield, Grover & Vedlitz (2008), "Identifying the impact of the built environment on flood damage in Texas," *Disasters* 32(1):1–18. County property damage from 423 flood events, 1997–2001. Built-environment measures were **impervious surface**, wetland alteration and dams, with biophysical and socioeconomic controls. — [PubMed 18217915](https://www.pubmed.gov/18217915/); [typeset PDF](https://typeset.io/pdf/identifying-the-impact-of-the-built-environment-on-flood-3urisk8xsd.pdf)
- A later Texas coastal-watershed dissertation reports that a higher percentage of impervious surface increases flood damage. A widely quoted Brody figure (~$4,000 of added flood damage per m² of pavement per flood) comes from an interview, **not** a peer-reviewed estimate. — [search summary, core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/79653809.pdf); [HPB Magazine](https://www.hpbmagazine.org/?p=1996)
- Gourevitch et al. (2023), *Nature Climate Change* 13:250–257: using the property-level **First Street Foundation** flood model, flood-exposed US residential property is overvalued by $121–237B. Overvaluation concentrates in coastal counties without flood-disclosure laws. This is a property-value-at-risk exposure measure, but it is a recent snapshot. — [RFF](https://www.rff.org/publications/journal-articles/unpriced-climate-risk-and-the-potential-consequences-of-overvaluation-in-us-housing-markets/); [Zenodo data](https://zenodo.org/records/7420416)
- USACE **National Structure Inventory (NSI)** gives point-level structures with depreciated replacement value. USACE says county or other large aggregates are reasonably accurate on average, but individual attributes may not be. NSI 2022 population was estimated for 2020 using 2010 block and 2020 county data. — [GEE community catalog NSI](https://gee-community-catalog.org/projects/nsi/); [USACE NSI metadata on GitHub](https://github.com/GitLutz/NSI); [USACE NSI Q&A webinar Jan 2026](https://planning.erdc.dren.mil/toolbox/webinars/23Jan26-NSI_QA.pdf)
- Davenport, Burke & Diffenbaugh (2021, *PNAS*): about 36% (95% CI 20–46%) of 1988–2017 US flood damages ($75B of $199B) is attributable to historical precipitation change. The method was state-month fixed-effects panels on more than 6,600 damage reports. The remaining variation reflects exposure and vulnerability plus noise, which is context for how much X can explain. — [PMC 7848586](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/7848586/); [Stanford news](https://news.stanford.edu/2021/01/11/climate-change-caused-one-third-historical-flood-damages/)

### Inferences
- For a county × month model, the strongest exposure feature the literature points to is **housing units in the FEMA SFHA**, ideally interacted with or alongside total housing units. It can be built from an NFHL overlay on census blocks or building footprints/NSI, held fixed in time or scaled by the census housing trend.
- Annual **NLCD developed share and mean impervious %** per county (Annual NLCD 1985–2023) is the one exposure series that varies year to year across the full 2000–2023 window. It is a natural complement to decennial and ACS housing counts.
- NSI and First Street are cross-sectional (around 2020–2022). Using them as time-invariant county features is fine, but they partly embed post-2016 development, a mild look-ahead leak for the 2017–2023 test split. Disclose this or use them only as static features.

### Gaps
- No full-text R² or variance-decomposition numbers were obtainable for any exposure variable in county SHELDUS models, because fetch was blocked.
- The direction and size of the impervious-surface coefficient in Brody et al. (2008) was not confirmed from snippets.
- First Street county aggregate availability and licensing were not confirmed.

## Q2. Vulnerability / social variables (SVI, income, mobile homes, building age)

### Takeaway
Evidence that social vulnerability predicts **property** loss is mixed. Tellman et al. (2020) find county damage is higher in rural, poorer, lower-house-value counties. Rufat et al. (2019) find SVI-type indices explain property loss poorly. Individual components (poverty, median home value, rurality) beat composite indices. No sourced evidence on mobile homes or building age specific to property loss was found.

### Cited Findings
- Tellman et al. (2020): models with **individual sociodemographic factors** outperform the composite SoVI. Variables tied to damage differ from those tied to deaths. Damage is higher in rural counties, counties with lower median house values, lower housing density (people per home), higher poverty, and higher shares of Black, Hispanic and Native American residents below the poverty line. — [MDPI 2071-1050/12/15/6006](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/12/15/6006); [USU](https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/envs_facpub/1612)
- Rufat, Tate, Emrich & Antolini (2019), "How Valid Are Social Vulnerability Models?" *Annals of the AAG* 109(4):1131–1153. Using Hurricane Sandy outcomes, the indices best explained housing-assistance applicants but **poorly explained property loss**. — [Princeton collaborate](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/how-valid-are-social-vulnerability-models-3/); [U Iowa IRO](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/How-Valid-Are-Social-Vulnerability-Models/9983984517102771)
- Gourevitch et al. (2023): low-income households face more equity-loss risk from flood-risk repricing. This links income to the consequence, not to direct damage. — [RFF press release](https://www.rff.org/news/press-releases/us-housing-market-overvalued-by-200-billion-due-to-unpriced-climate-risks/)
- **CDC/ATSDR SVI** releases: 2000, 2010, 2014, 2016, 2018, 2020, 2022. ACS 5-year data has been used since 2010, so releases contain overlapping sample years. County rankings exist for 2010, 2014, 2016 and 2018 (per documentation) and for 2020. County coverage for 2000 and 2022 was not confirmed from CDC documentation in snippets. Field names changed between 2016 and 2018 and again between 2020 and 2022. Tract boundaries differ across vintages. — [ATSDR SVI download page](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/place-health/php/svi/svi-data-documentation-download.html); [SVI 2018 documentation](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/place-health/media/pdfs/2024/08/SVI2018Documentation_01192022_1.pdf); [SVI 2022 documentation](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/place-health/media/pdfs/2024/10/SVI2022Documentation.pdf)

### Inferences
- Use **raw components** (median household income, poverty %, median home value, % mobile homes, % structures built before 1980, rural/urban) from NHGIS decennial (2000, 2010, 2020) and ACS 5-year (2009+) data, rather than the composite SVI. This matches Tellman's finding and avoids SVI vintage and methodology breaks. Mobile-home share and year-structure-built are standard census housing tables (2000 SF3; ACS B25024 and B25034), so they line up with the existing NHGIS pipeline. That is a dataset fact from general knowledge, not from an opened source.
- Low median home value is associated with *higher* normalized damage in Tellman et al., but absolute dollar loss scales with value. The sign depends on whether the target is normalized. For raw SHELDUS dollars, include value as exposure and income/poverty as vulnerability.

### Gaps
- No sourced study isolating **mobile-home share** or **building age** effects on county property flood loss was found in the snippets.
- No sourced R² for SVI in a SHELDUS property-loss model.

## Q3. Adaptation / mitigation variables (NFIP take-up, CRS, HMA buyouts, levees, dams, wetlands)

### Takeaway
The Texas A&M (Brody/Highfield/Zahran) literature finds that **CRS participation and specific CRS activities**, **open-space protection** and **intact wetlands** are associated with lower flood losses, while **wetland alteration** increases damage. CRS effects in some studies appear only at high CRS classes (Class 5 or better). Buyouts show positive loss-avoidance ROI in FEMA case studies. NFIP take-up is itself a reaction to recent floods (Gallagher 2014), which is exactly the C → A path in the project's DAG.

### Cited Findings
- **Wetlands, Florida:** Brody, Zahran, Maghelal, Grover & Highfield (2007), "The Rising Costs of Floods," *JAPA* 73(3):330–345, DOI 10.1080/01944360708977981. Using 383 non-hurricane flood events in Florida counties, 1997–2001, alteration of naturally occurring wetlands significantly increases flood property damage, all else equal. The study also examined impervious surface and dams. — [APA knowledge base](https://planning.org/knowledgebase/resource/9200805); [Scilit](https://www.scilit.net/publications/53c3f00d3c4a28050fb9f762dc022302); [TAMU JAPA best-paper note](https://archcomm.arch.tamu.edu/archive/news/spring2008/stories/japaPaper.html)
- **Wetlands, Texas:** Brody et al. (2008, *Disasters*). Using 423 county flood events, 1997–2001, naturally occurring wetlands play an important role in mitigating flood damage. — [PubMed 18217915](https://www.pubmed.gov/18217915/)
- **Section 404 permits:** Brody et al. (2007), *Natural Hazards* 40(2):413–428. Wetland-alteration permits under Clean Water Act §404 were geo-referenced and correlated with stream-gauge flooding. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/nathaz/v40y2007i2p413-428.html)
- **National wetland loss and NFIP claims:** a 2026 study, summarized by EDF, estimates wetland loss since 1985 increased residential NFIP claim payments by about $10.1B. This is an advocacy-group summary and the authors were not verified. — [EDF blog, June 2026](https://blogs.edf.org/markets/2026/06/01/new-research-shows-whats-at-stake-when-wetlands-disappear.md)
- **CRS:** Highfield & Brody (2013), "Evaluating the effectiveness of local mitigation activities in reducing flood losses," *Natural Hazards Review* 14(4):229–236. Highfield, Brody & Blessing (2014), *Natural Hazards* 74(2):687–704, DOI 10.1007/s11069-014-1209-1: parcel-level test of whether CRS activities reduced insured losses in the Clear Creek watershed, TX. Highfield & Brody (2017), "Determining the effects of the FEMA Community Rating System program on flood losses in the United States," *IJDRR* 21:396–404. — [Highfield CV, TAMUG](https://www.tamug.edu/mars/cv/WesleyHighfield.pdf); [Springer DOI](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11069-014-1209-1); [InsuranceNewsNet summary](https://insurancenewsnet.com/oarticle/studies-from-texas-am-university-update-current-data-on-disaster-risk-reduction-determining-the-effects-of-the-fema-community-rating-system)
- **Open space:** Brody & Highfield (2013), *Land Use Policy*, a national study of open-space protection and flood losses. Cited only; findings not retrieved. — [search result listing references](https://le.uwpress.org/content/94/2/175/tab-references)
- **CRS, take-up and claims (Gulf):** Frimpong, Petrolia, Harri & Cartwright (2019/2020, *Applied Economic Perspectives & Policy* 42(2):245–262). CRS participation significantly raises NFIP uptake, and the effect generally increases with class. **Reduced damage claims appear only for very high participation (Class 5).** — [AgEcon Search](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/284989/files/Manuscript_Frimpong_19-1.pdf); [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wly:apecpp:v:42:y:2020:i:2:p:245-262)
- **CRS mechanics:** Class 10 = non-participating. Premium discounts come in 5% increments per class. Class changes take effect on May 1 and October 1. FEMA publishes an abridged CRS dataset, updated semiannually. — [data.gov CRS dataset](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/national-flood-insurance-program-nfip-community-rating-system-dataset-bd904); [FEMA Region 5 CRS fact sheet 2012 (archived)](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20161130040841/https://www.fema.gov/media-library-data/20130726-1842-25045-0682/region5_crs_may_2012_508.pdf)
- **NFIP claims drivers:** Kousky & Michel-Kerjan (*Journal of Risk and Insurance*, DOI 10.1111/jori.12106) examined more than 1M NFIP claims, 1978–2012, with fixed effects for flood zone, house characteristics and mitigation. Specific results were not retrieved. — [vLex](https://law-journals-books.vlex.com/vid/examining-flood-insurance-claims-855633281); [zendy](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/jori.12106)
- **Take-up responds to floods (endogeneity):** Gallagher (2014), *AEJ: Applied* 6(3):206–233. NFIP take-up spikes the year after a flood (about 9–10%) and decays to baseline over about 15 years. Non-flooded communities in the same TV market respond at one-third the rate. — [Gallagher PDF, Montana State](https://www.montana.edu/gallagher/documents/flood_insurance_aeja.pdf); [AEA research highlight](https://www.aeaweb.org/research/will-flood-insurance-takeup-spike); [openICPSR replication](https://www.openicpsr.org/openicpsr/project/113898/version/V1/view)
- **Buyouts (HMA):** in FEMA loss-avoidance studies, 12 Iowa communities avoided $98.7M in losses in 2008 floods, with a BCR of 2.19. Birmingham, AL had 735 acquisitions, $63.7M avoided against a $43.3M cost (ROI 1.46). Some cases fall below break-even, such as Southampton Co., VA. — [AIR Worldwide blog](https://www.air-worldwide.com/blog/posts/2014/4/floodplain-acquisition-programs-can-reduce-future-losses/); [FEMA Birmingham case study](https://fema.gov/case-study/loss-avoidance-study-birmingham-al-acquisitions); [VDEM Southampton](https://vaemergency.gov/aem/grants/loss-avoidance-studies/1-loss-avoidance-study-southampton.pdf)
- Buyout adoption is itself selected: counties with higher population and income are more likely to administer buyouts (Mach et al. 2019, *Science Advances*), and there are racial disparities (Elliott et al. 2020). — [Mach et al. 2019](https://www.science.org/doi/pdf/10.1126/sciadv.aax8995); [Elliott et al. 2020](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/2378023120905439)

### Inferences
- In the DAG W ← C → A → Y, CRS class, NFIP take-up and cumulative buyouts are all plausibly caused by past flood experience (Gallagher; Mach et al.). They must be measured **lagged**, as of the start of the year or the prior year, to stay pre-treatment for the month being predicted.
- Expected signs from the literature: CRS class (better class → lower loss, possibly nonlinear with a threshold near Class 5), wetland share (more wetland → lower), wetland loss (more loss → higher), cumulative acquisitions (→ lower). For NFIP policies per housing unit, the effect on *SHELDUS* loss is ambiguous: it signals both risk and adaptation.
- Levees and dams are mostly time-invariant over 2000–2023. Use them as static features (leveed-area share of county, count of dams), mindful that a levee can raise exposure behind it (the "levee effect"). This is a known concept, but no source was retrieved here.

### Gaps
- Magnitudes from Highfield & Brody 2013/2017 (for example, percent claims reduction per CRS class step) were not retrieved.
- Brody & Highfield 2013 open-space findings were not retrieved.
- No source was found on the direct effect of levees or the National Levee Database on county losses.

## Q4. Datasets: coverage, resolution, county availability, access, alignment to 2000–2023

### Takeaway
Fully time-varying county series covering 2000–2023 are available for **Annual NLCD land cover and impervious surface** (annual), **NFIP policies in force** (derived daily from OpenFEMA policy records), **HMA mitigated properties** (by project date) and **census housing and income** (decennial plus ACS). CRS class history, SVI, NSI, NLD and First Street are periodic snapshots or need assembly.

### Cited Findings
- **Annual NLCD Collection 1.x (USGS):** annual land cover and **fractional impervious surface**, among 6 products, for CONUS from 1985–2023, with a later release adding 2024–2025. Sources disagree on the end year by release: Collection 1.0 runs 1985–2023, and Collection 1.2 adds 2025. This supersedes legacy NLCD epochs (2001, 2004, 2006, 2008, 2011, 2013, 2016, 2019, 2021; those years were not confirmed from snippets). — [USGS Annual NLCD](https://www.usgs.gov/centers/eros/science/annual-national-land-cover-database?page=1); [data.gov Annual NLCD land cover](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/annual-national-land-cover-database-nlcd-collection-1-land-cover); [USGS EROS Collection 1.2](https://www.usgs.gov/centers/eros/science/usgs-eros-archive-land-cover-annual-nlcd-collection-12-land-cover); [MRLC](https://www.mrlc.gov/node/1239)
- **OpenFEMA FimaNfipPolicies (FIMA NFIP Redacted Policies v2):** historic and current policies. Policies in force are derived by filtering on policy effective and termination dates. The dataset is filterable by `countyCode` (5-digit FIPS), updated about monthly, and lags the system of record. Policy *contracts* in force approximate insured structures, while policies in force count condo units. — [FEMA OpenFEMA page](https://www.fema.gov/ht/node/694076); [UI climateapi get_nfip_policies](https://ui-research.github.io/climateapi/reference/get_nfip_policies.html); [mebauer nfip-datasets](https://github.com/mebauer/nfip-datasets); [rfema vignette](https://ropensci.r-universe.dev/rfema/doc/getting_started.html)

Not every listed property was ultimately mitigated. — [data.gov HMA Mitigated Properties](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/hazard-mitigation-assistance-mitigated-properties); [FEMA OpenFEMA changelog](https://fema.gov/about/openfema/changelog)

Completeness is a known caveat. — [Addresscloud NLD note](https://help.addresscloud.com/article/131-data-usanld); [Army.mil update 2018](https://www.army.mil/article/206422/updated_national_levee_database_now_available); [Apify screener (third party)](https://apify.com/malonestar/usace-levee-flood-risk-screener)
- **USACE NSI:** CONUS, Alaska and Hawaii point structures with depreciated replacement value. 2022 vintage. Aggregates to county are reasonably accurate. — [GEE catalog NSI](https://gee-community-catalog.org/projects/nsi/); [NSI metadata](https://github.com/GitLutz/NSI)

## Q5. Endogeneity / leakage risks

### Takeaway
**NFIP claims (FimaNfipClaims), Individual Assistance/Public Assistance amounts, disaster-declaration counts and HMA funding triggered by declarations are post-event or loss-derived and must not be contemporaneous inputs.** Policies in force, CRS class, and buyout stock are pre-event state variables, but they are causally downstream of past floods. Use them lagged; they are legitimate A measures.

### Cited Findings
- NFIP take-up rises about 10% the year after a flood and decays over about 15 years. Insurance is therefore a function of recent losses. — [Gallagher 2014 PDF](https://www.montana.edu/gallagher/documents/flood_insurance_aeja.pdf); [AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/research/will-flood-insurance-takeup-spike)
- Post-loss buyers tend to let coverage lapse after 2–4 years. — [search summary, Florida OIR report](https://www.floir.gov/docs-sf/default-source/property-and-casualty/other-property-casualty-reports/final-report.pdf)
- Buyouts are administered more in counties with higher population and income, and are funded after declared disasters (HMGP). — [Mach et al. 2019](https://www.science.org/doi/pdf/10.1126/sciadv.aax8995)
- SHELDUS splits multi-county event totals evenly across affected counties when only totals are known. This is measurement error in Y that loss-derived inputs would share. — [search summary of NY Fed SR1135](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr1135.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Must NOT be inputs (loss-derived or post-event):** NFIP claims counts and payouts; FEMA IA/PA obligations; disaster declarations in the same or later month; HMA *grants* dated after an event in the target window; SHELDUS-derived lags (forbidden by the project rule). Also First Street and NSI attributes that may embed damage history (for example, NSI is partly built from post-event data, unverified). Repetitive-loss property counts are claims-derived and also forbidden.
- **OK as A (lagged, pre-period):** NFIP policies or contracts in force per HU as of the prior month or year; CRS class as of the prior May/Oct; cumulative mitigated properties to the prior year; wetland and impervious share in the prior year; static levee and dam features.
- Because A sits on C → A → Y, including A lets the model separate "high-risk but adapted" counties from "high-risk unadapted" ones. Lagged A still carries information about historical flood frequency (C), which is desirable for prediction but should not be interpreted causally.

### Gaps
- No source was found quantifying leakage bias from using NFIP claims as predictors of SHELDUS loss. The recommendation above is reasoning, not a sourced result.
