## Q1. Non-loss measures of long-run flood climate, and which exist before 2000

### Takeaway
The best-documented US example of a non-loss flood-climate measure is Davenport et al. (2021). They use extreme-precipitation exceedance built from gridded precipitation, analysed in a state-month fixed-effects panel. NOAA nClimGrid supports this kind of measure well before 2000: monthly data from 1895 and daily data from 1951. USGS annual peak-flow records (NWIS, Bulletin 17C) cover many decades and give a streamflow-based alternative. FEMA NRI riverine frequency is built from NCEI Storm Events for roughly 1996 onward. That source overlaps with SHELDUS's own inputs and with the 2000–2023 study window, so it is a poor choice for C.

### Cited Findings
- Davenport, Burke & Diffenbaugh (2021), "Contribution of historical precipitation change to US flood damages," PNAS 118(4), e2017524118, DOI 10.1073/pnas.2017524118. They used more than 6,600 state-level flood damage reports and fixed-effects panel regressions at the state-month level to link precipitation to damages. They attribute about 36% of 1988–2017 US flood damages, about $73B (95% CI $39–91B), to precipitation change. Climate models indicate that anthropogenic forcing raised the odds of exceeding the extreme-precipitation thresholds responsible for most damage. — [Search summary / ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2021/01/210111190141.htm); [PMC record](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7848586/) (not opened, blocked); [CA Water Library record](https://cawaterlibrary.net/document/contribution-of-historical-precipitation-change-to-us-flood-damages/)
- Press coverage reports different total-damage denominators (about $199B in one outlet), so the dollar totals should be checked against the paper. — [Harvard SITN summary](https://sites.harvard.edu/sitn/2021/02/12/climate-change-tied-to-a-third-of-recent-flood-damage-in-u-s)
- NOAA nClimGrid-Daily gives gridded daily precipitation and temperature for CONUS from 1951 to present, at about 5 km resolution. It is interpolated from GHCN-Daily stations with thin-plate smoothing splines. Monthly nClimGrid begins in 1895. — [data.gov catalog](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/noaa-nclimgrid-daily-version-1-daily-gridded-temperature-and-precipitation-for-the-co-1951); [Drought.gov](https://drought.gov/data-maps-tools/gridded-climate-datasets-noaas-nclimgrid-daily); [GEE community catalog](https://gee-community-catalog.org/projects/nclim_grid/)
- NOAA/NCICS publishes an "analysis-ready" county-scale nClimGrid derivative (EpiNOAA). It includes monthly and decadal county CSVs and a period-of-record county file for 1951 to present. — [NCICS ARC](https://arc.ncics.org/nclimgrid-overview/)
- USGS flood frequency follows Bulletin 17C, which fits a log-Pearson Type III distribution to annual peak flows. Annual peaks are downloadable from NWIS (nwis.waterdata.usgs.gov/usa/nwis/peak) in PeakFQ format. Recent USGS datasets use peaks from water years 1924–2022. Bulletin 17C assumes stationarity, and USGS studies screen for trends and change points first. — [Bulletin 17C, USGS TM 4-B5](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/tm4B5); [USGS flood frequency reports](https://www.usgs.gov/science/flood-frequency-reports); [USGS SIR 2025-5019 (MT/ND/SD/WY)](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/sir20255019); [Illinois 464-gage 2017 analysis](https://catalog-beta.data.gov/dataset/peak-flow-frequency-analysis-for-464-u-s-geological-survey-streamgages-in-illinois-in-2017)
- FEMA NRI riverine-flooding annualized frequency is "the average number of recorded Riverine Flooding hazard occurrences (event-days) per year over the period of record," cited as 24 years. The source is the NCEI Storm Events Database, and v1.18 added three more years. Riverine frequency is computed at county level, and tracts and blocks inherit the county value. A minimum-value rule stops zeros from wiping out the multiplicative EAL. — [FEMA NRI Annualized Frequency](https://hazards.fema.gov/nri/annualized-frequency); [NRI Riverine Flooding](https://hazards.fema.gov/nri/riverine-flooding); [NRI Data Version documentation](https://FEMA.Gov/sites/default/files/documents/fema_national-risk-index-data-version-and-update-documentation.pdf); [NRI Technical Documentation v1.18](https://hazards.fema.gov/nri/Content/StaticDocuments/DataDownload/Archive/v118_0/fema_national-risk-index_technical-documentation.pdf) (none opened; host blocked)

### Inferences
- **Recommended primary C option:** compute county-level climatology from nClimGrid (daily from 1951 or monthly from 1895) for a pre-period such as 1951–1999. Candidate metrics are (a) the share of months with precipitation above the county's own 95th or 99th percentile, or above a fixed absolute threshold, (b) the mean annual-maximum 1-day, 3-day or 5-day precipitation, and (c) a GEV-fitted 10-year or 100-year return level. These mirror Davenport et al.'s exceedance-threshold approach. They are non-loss, available before 2000, and do not overlap with SHELDUS.
- **Secondary C option:** a USGS peak-flow climatology. Use gages in or draining the county with at least 30 pre-2000 peaks, and take the median Q100/Q2 ratio, the coefficient of variation of the annual peaks, or the count of years above the flood-stage discharge. The data are spotty: some counties have no gage, so routing by HUC or nearest gage is needed. Regulation and nonstationarity also complicate it.
- FEMA NRI riverine frequency should not be used as C in a 2000–2023 study. It draws on NCEI Storm Events from about 1996, which is also a SHELDUS input, so it shares source and time window with Y. Its tracts also inherit the county value, so it gives no within-county information.
- NOAA Atlas 14 return levels are a convenient pre-computed option. They are non-loss and based on station records largely ending in the 2000s. Some post-2000 data may enter, which is a mild overlap.

## Q2. Physical susceptibility: floodplain share, terrain, HAND, soils, wetlands

### Takeaway
Wing et al. (2018) and Bates et al. (2021) supply CONUS-wide 30 m hazard maps (Fathom / First Street) that support a county "share of land, or of assets, in the 1% floodplain." These maps find about 3x more people exposed than FEMA regulatory maps. HAND (Nobre et al.) is a cheap DEM-based inundation proxy, but I found no study linking it to county losses. Texas A&M work by Brody and Highfield finds that the 100-year floodplain is a weak predictor of losses, while impervious surface and wetland alteration matter.

### Cited Findings
- Wing, Bates, Smith, Sampson, Johnson, Fargione & Morefield (2018), "Estimates of present and future flood risk in the conterminous United States," Environ. Res. Lett. 13, 034023, DOI 10.1088/1748-9326/aaac65. It is a 30 m, 2D hydraulic model of all of CONUS. About 41M Americans are exposed to the 1-in-100-year fluvial or pluvial flood, versus about 13M under FEMA maps. The model reports about 90% hazard skill against detailed local models. LA, FL, AZ and WV are disproportionately exposed, with 1/4 to 1/3 of assets on the floodplain. — [IOP news](https://ioppublishing.org/news/usa-flood-risk-vastly-underestimated-study-shows/); [Fathom page](https://www.fathom.global/academic-papers/estimates-of-present-and-future-flood-risk-in-the-conterminous-united-states/); [PDF mirror](https://biotech.law.lsu.edu/blog/Wing_et_al-2018-Environmental_Research_Letters.pdf)
- Bates et al. (2021), "Combined Modeling of US Fluvial, Pluvial, and Coastal Flood Hazard Under Current and Future Climates," Water Resour. Res. 57(2), e2020WR028673, DOI 10.1029/2020WR028673. It models fluvial, pluvial and coastal hazard at 30 m and includes local flood defences. Validation against local models and FEMA 1% maps gives a Critical Success Index of about 0.69–0.82. Projections to 2035 and 2050 show hazard flat or falling in the center of the country. The data are public through First Street's Flood Factor. — [NSF PAR record](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10263939-combined-modeling-us-fluvial-pluvial-coastal-flood-hazard-under-current-future-climates); [MIT PDF mirror](https://texmex.mit.edu/ftp/pub/emanuel/PAPERS/Bates_etal_2021.pdf); [Fathom page](https://www.fathom.global/academic-papers/combined-modeling-of-us-fluvial-pluvial-and-coastal-flood-hazard-under-current-and-future-climates/)
- HAND (height above nearest drainage) is each cell's height above the stream cell it drains to. Nobre et al. (2016), "HAND contour: a new proxy predictor of inundation extent," Hydrological Processes, DOI 10.1002/hyp.10581, use it to map potential inundation without flood observations. NOAA's 2023 evaluation (Aristizabal et al.) notes nearest-flowpath limitations on skill. — [Zendy record](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/hyp.10581); [NOAA repository](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/52493/noaa_52493_DS1.pdf); [HESS 2024](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/28/1287/2024/)
- Brody & Highfield et al. (2008, *Disasters*), "Identifying the impact of the built environment on flood damage in Texas," covers 423 flood events in Texas counties from 1997 to 2001. Higher impervious surface was associated with more damage, after controlling for biophysical and socioeconomic factors and for wetland alteration and dams. — [Typeset PDF](https://typeset.io/pdf/identifying-the-impact-of-the-built-environment-on-flood-3urisk8xsd.pdf); [record](https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/874408632)
- Brody and Highfield (2013) report that the 100-year floodplain is a poor indicator of likely flood damage, and that many losses occur outside it. — [TAMU news](https://newsarchive.arch.tamu.edu/news/2013/5/29/floodplain-losspredictor/)

### Inferences
- **Recommended susceptibility block, all static and non-loss:**
  - Share of county area, or of building footprints, in the Fathom/First Street 1% floodplain. Avoid FEMA NFHL as the primary measure, because its coverage is incomplete and it under-maps, especially in rural Midwest counties.
  - Median or low-quantile HAND, or the share of land with HAND below 5 m. HAND can be computed from USGS 3DEP or SRTM, or taken from NOAA OWP HAND products.
  - Mean slope.
  - Drainage density, from NHDPlus.
  - Share of soils in hydrologic soil groups C/D, from SSURGO/gNATSGO.
  - Wetland share, from NWI or NLCD.
  - Impervious share, from NLCD 2001. This is the earliest NLCD and is nearly pre-period.
- The First Street and Fathom maps are produced in 2018–2021 and include flood defences. They therefore partly embed adaptation (A), so conditioning on them partly conditions on a mediator. Terrain, soils and HAND are cleaner pre-treatment variables.

### Gaps
- No source found links HAND to US county-level losses. Its relation to losses is unverified.
- No search covered evidence on SSURGO soil group, stream density, or 3DEP slope as loss predictors.
- I did not open the Brody/Highfield coefficient tables.

## Q3. Causal methods for disaster losses: confounding by climate and adaptation

### Takeaway
The canonical approach, reviewed in Hsiang (2016), takes weather intensity as quasi-random within unit after unit and time fixed effects. It then models adaptation as an interaction between intensity and long-run climate. Hsiang & Narita (2012) find that marginal cyclone damage falls in places with more intense storm climatologies, which is exactly the W×C → loss heterogeneity in τ(w; x, c). Applications of double machine learning and causal forests to US flood losses exist mainly for mitigation programmes, not for hazard intensity.

### Cited Findings
- Hsiang, S. (2016), "Climate Econometrics," Annual Review of Resource Economics 8:43–75 (NBER WP 22181). It covers nonlinear models, spatial and temporal displacement, measuring adaptation, and projection. — [NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22181); [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:anr:reseco:v:8:y:2016:p:43-75); [eScholarship PDF](https://escholarship.org/content/qt50t4t5j9/qt50t4t5j9.pdf)
- Mérel & Gammans (2018) argue that panel weather estimates reflect long-run (adapted) responses only when cross-sectional climate variation dominates. — [AgEcon abstracts](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/274399/files/Abstracts_18_05_23_22_14_42_82__169_237_57_250_0.pdf) (secondary)
- Hsiang & Narita (2012), "Adaptation to cyclone risk: evidence from the global cross-section," Climate Change Economics 3(2), 1250011. They reconstruct all tropical cyclones from 1950 to 2008. A 1 m/s stronger wind raises damages by 20.3%. Each 1 m/s rise in climatological (average) exposure lowers that marginal effect to 19.6%. The effect is small at the margin but large for countries with long histories of intense storms. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wsi/ccexxx/v03y2012i02ns201000781250011x.html); [IfW Kiel PDF](https://ifw-kiel.de/fileadmin/Dateiverwaltung/IfW-Publications/fis-import/4919841b-2ab8-42ad-b905-28c4adb09337-climate-change-economics.pdf)
- Bakkensen & Mendelsohn (2016, JAERE), "Risk and Adaptation: Evidence from Global Hurricane Damages and Fatalities," argue that Hsiang–Narita's framework implies adaptation cuts long-run damage by only about 3%. Their own estimates say adaptation reduces impacts by more than an order of magnitude. Conflict: the two papers disagree sharply on the size of adaptation. — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ucp/jaerec/doi10.1086-685908.html); [NCSU working paper](https://cenrep.ncsu.edu/cenrep/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/Bakkensen.pdf)
- Davenport et al. (2021) use state-month panel fixed effects to identify the precipitation→damage relationship from within-state weather variation, as described in Q1. — [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2021/01/210111190141.htm)
- CausalFlow (arXiv 2312.03843) estimates the effect of the NFIP Community Rating System with a deep generative causal model. Its covariates include precipitation and flood risk, and it reports savings of about $5k–15k per household. It finds that efficacy varies significantly with income, population, precipitation and flood risk, among other factors. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.03843); [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2312.03843) (abstract only)
- A 2026 study of FEMA flood-mitigation grants covers 506 high-risk counties. It uses double machine learning with a Mundlak adjustment, causal forests and K-means, and finds that loss reductions vary systematically with county context. — [search result: NeurIPS 2026 listing](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/174619) (abstract only; authors and details unverified)

### Inferences
- **Mapping to the project's DAG:** W ← C → A → Y.
  - Option 1 is to include county fixed effects. They absorb time-invariant C and A, but then C-heterogeneity is identifiable only through W×C interactions, which is the Hsiang–Narita design.
  - Option 2 is to condition on non-loss C and static susceptibility X in DML or a causal forest, with τ(w; x, c) estimated as heterogeneity along c.
  - Under either option, C must be measured before the period and from sources independent of Y. The current SHELDUS-based C is mechanically correlated with Y's measurement process (reporting practices and thresholds), which is a further reason for the instructor's requirement.
- Positivity matters. Low-C counties rarely see high W, so the overlap of W across C bins should be checked before interpreting τ at high w in low-c counties.

## Q4. Levee effect, safe development paradox, and adaptation lowering damage per unit hazard

### Takeaway
The conceptual framing comes from Di Baldassarre et al. (2018). A 2024 systematic review found that 75% of 94 studies support the levee effect or safe development paradox, though about 40% of them examine only exposure. Kreibich et al. (2022) show, using 45 paired events, that risk management usually lowers impacts for comparable events but fails for unprecedented ones. That implies adaptation flattens the damage function up to a design threshold, after which damages jump.

### Cited Findings
- Di Baldassarre et al. (2018), "HESS Opinions: An interdisciplinary research agenda to explore the unintended consequences of structural flood protection," HESS 22, 5629–5637. It frames the levee effect, safe development paradox or safety dilemma: structural protection lowers flood probability, but it encourages development and complacency, raising potential losses. It is a research agenda, not new empirical evidence. — [HESS](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/22/5629/2018/)
- Fusinato et al. (2024), "Safe development paradox: evidence and methodological insights from a systematic review," Natural Hazards, DOI 10.1007/s11069-024-06774-z. It reviews 94 studies from 2001 to 2023. 75% gave conclusive evidence of the safe development paradox or levee effect, through three mechanisms: development in protected areas, reduced preparedness, and increased risk. About 40% of studies examined exposure only, and non-structural adaptation is under-studied. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11069-024-06774-z) (not opened); [UFRGS repository](https://lume.ufrgs.br/handle/10183/288474)
- Kreibich et al. (2022), "The challenge of unprecedented floods and droughts in risk management," Nature 608, 80–86, DOI 10.1038/s41586-022-04917-5. It analyses 45 paired events in the same area. Risk management generally reduced impacts, but when the second event was much more hazardous, impacts were almost always higher, because management "was not designed to deal with such extreme events." Only two cases had lower impact despite higher hazard, credited to better governance and high investment in integrated management. — [Lund repository](https://lup.lub.lu.se/record/a6a9b4e5-0910-4d1d-803f-0e353caea896); [ObsEbre summary](https://obsebre.es/en/news/1112-a-new-study-on-the-challenge-of-unprecedented-floods-and-droughts-in-risk-management-has-been-published-in-the-journal-nature)
- Hsiang & Narita (2012) find that marginal damages from wind intensity fall with climatological exposure, as described in Q3. — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wsi/ccexxx/v03y2012i02ns201000781250011x.html)

### Inferences
- **Expected shape of τ(w; x, c):** the literature suggests that in high-C (adapted) counties, loss is lower per unit W at moderate W. At extreme W, beyond design levels, the effect may be similar or larger, because of the levee effect and extra exposure behind protection. A causal forest or a flexible W×C spline should therefore allow a crossing or threshold pattern, not just a monotone interaction.
- For Midwest relevance: the 1993 and 2008 Upper Mississippi floods and levee development are classic levee-effect settings. No search here confirmed a specific quantitative study of them.

- I could not open the Kousky papers (for example, on NFIP and levees). None were retrieved.
