# Wet soils, not just storms, drive Midwest flood losses

The literature supports a compact, leakage-safe design for this project. The hazard block (W) should combine a monthly precipitation total with a multi-day maximum such as Rx5day, expressed as exceedances of each county's own pre-2000 percentiles. Antecedent soil moisture, snowmelt and frozen ground must be added, because they are first-order Midwest flood generators that rainfall alone misses. The flood-climate block (C) should come from non-loss, pre-2000 records: nClimGrid or PRISM precipitation climatologies and USGS peak-flow statistics. FEMA NRI or anything built from NCEI Storm Events should not be used for C, because SHELDUS draws on the same source. Exposure (X) is best proxied by housing units in high-risk floodplains plus annual NLCD impervious and developed share. Adaptation (A) can be measured with lagged NFIP policies in force, CRS class, cumulative HMA buyouts and wetland share. The best-supported model is a two-part hurdle (occurrence, then log-severity) fitted with gradient-boosted trees and with a BYM2 spatial model in R-INLA, which pools sparse county histories. These should be benchmarked against a county × calendar-month climatology and scored on PR-AUC, Brier skill, Spearman ρ and loss captured in the top-k counties. Two caveats apply throughout. No published study fits exactly this problem (SHELDUS county-month losses with ranked hazard drivers or a BYM2 hurdle), so the plan assembles adjacent evidence. And nearly every source was read only as an abstract or search snippet: numbers marked **[snippet]** or **[unverified]** need checking before citation.

## Precipitation explains about a third of US flood damage, concentrated in the extreme wet tail

The anchor study for W is Davenport, Burke and Diffenbaugh (2021, PNAS). It uses a state × month fixed-effects panel of more than 6,600 damage reports. **Both monthly precipitation and 5-day maximum precipitation had independent, significant positive effects on damage.** The extremely wet upper quantiles are "responsible for most flood damages." Historical precipitation change accounts for **about 36% (95% CI 20–46%) of 1988–2017 US flood damage, roughly $73B of about $199B** [snippet; one summary gives $75B, so check the denominator] ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/7848586/); [Stanford News](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2021/01/climate-change-caused-one-third-historical-flood-damages)). The group's earlier abstract reports a **log-linear damage–precipitation relationship** whose strength varies by region ([ADS](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019AGUFMGC23A..02D)). That supports modelling log(1+loss), or the log-severity part of a hurdle, against precipitation percentiles rather than raw millimetres. The damage source is believed to be NCEI Storm Events but was not confirmed [unverified]. That matters because SHELDUS is derived from the same database.

Insurance-claims machine-learning work points the same way. Salas, Saha and Ravela found that adding Daymet rainfall improved NFIP-loss prediction in 11 counties, with XGBoost the best learner and **R² = 0.807** reported for coastal counties [snippet] ([arXiv 2212.08660](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2212.08660)). The iClaim random forest adds flood extent, river stage and water levels to precipitation. It reports **R² above 0.9 at the county/event level** [snippet] ([NOAA repository](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/64559)). A third NFIP study found that static hydrologic features, especially distance to stream and topographic wetness index, dominated ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/342973658_Predicting_flood_insurance_claims_with_hydrologic_and_socioeconomic_demographics_via_machine_learning_Exploring_the_roles_of_topography_minority_populations_and_political_dissimilarity)). That is a reminder that susceptibility features compete with W for explanatory power. Dutch claims data show rainfall intensity correlates more strongly with the *number* of claims than with their size ([Spekkers et al. 2013](https://www.hydrol-earth-syst-sci.net/17/913/2013/hess-17-913-2013-metrics.html)). This is direct support for letting W enter the occurrence part of the hurdle more strongly than the severity part. Pluvial flooding reportedly drove **60% of non-tropical-cyclone US flood claims** [snippet; original paper not identified] ([CAS catalog record](https://katalog.lib.cas.cz/KNAV/EdsRecord/edsair,edsair.doi...........4401c008941e0332c6a7e0609cebdf3d)). So local intense rainfall matters even away from mainstem rivers.

The decisive Midwest-specific finding is that **rainfall alone is a weak flood predictor**. Across 420 US catchments, soil-moisture-dependent precipitation excess, snowmelt and rain-on-snow explained flood timing and magnitude much better than rainfall characteristics ([Berghuijs et al. 2016](https://typeset.io/pdf/dominant-flood-generating-mechanisms-across-the-united-10tztxaz7l.pdf)). A CONUS machine-learning analysis found **snowmelt or rain-on-snow on saturated soil was the leading flood generator in the Midwest and Northeast** ([Hydrology Research 54(10)](https://iwaponline.com/hr/article/54/10/1181/97601/Unveiling-flood-generating-mechanisms-using)). Slater and Villarini identify both preceding precipitation and basin-wide wetness as critical drivers of US flood frequency ([GRL 2016](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/2016GL071199)). Midwest flood *frequency* has risen without larger peaks, consistent with more frequent heavy rain ([Mallakpour & Villarini 2015](https://www.nature.com/articles/nclimate2516)). March 2019 shows how these drivers combine. Autumn 2018 was the 12th-wettest October–December in 125 years ([NCEI](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/node/699)). Saturated soils then froze deeply ([ASDSO](https://damsafety.org/reference/2019-missouri-river-basin-historic-flood-event)). Rain on snowpack and frozen ground then produced rapid runoff ([Iowa DNR](https://iowadnr.gov/About-DNR/DNR-News-Releases/ArticleID/2384/Water-Summary-Update-Rapid-runoff-led-to-historic-flooding-in-March)). A model with only same-month county rainfall would miss that event, which falls in the 2017–2023 test window.

Two important gaps remain. **No study ranks precipitation, soil moisture and streamflow on SHELDUS county-month loss**, and none compares Rx1day, Rx3day, Rx5day and return-period exceedance head to head on US damage. Those comparisons are a legitimate contribution this project can make rather than inherit.

## Pre-2000 climate records make a clean, non-SHELDUS C possible

The project's DAG W ← C → A → Y requires C to be measured before the period and independently of Y. Several sources qualify. **PRISM monthly runs from 1895 and PRISM daily from 1981** at about 4 km. In Earth Engine the AN81 collections are deprecated in favour of `OREGONSTATE/PRISM/ANm` and `OREGONSTATE/PRISM/ANd`, and listed end dates conflict across catalog snapshots [check the live catalog] ([GEE ANd](https://developers.google.com/earth-engine/datasets/catalog/OREGONSTATE_PRISM_ANd?authuser=0); [GEE AN81m](https://developers.google.com/earth-engine/datasets/catalog/OREGONSTATE_PRISM_AN81m?hl=zh-tw)). **NOAA nClimGrid-Daily covers CONUS from 1951 at about 5 km**, with monthly data from 1895 ([data.gov](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/noaa-nclimgrid-daily-version-1-daily-gridded-temperature-and-precipitation-for-the-co-1951)). NCICS also publishes ready-made **county-level nClimGrid CSVs** ([NCICS ARC](https://arc.ncics.org/nclimgrid-overview/)), which removes the gridded-to-county aggregation step entirely. **USGS annual peak flows** reach back to water year 1924 at many gauges and support Bulletin 17C log-Pearson III flood-frequency fits ([USGS TM 4-B5](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/tm4B5)). Bulletin 17C assumes stationarity, which is questionable given rising Midwest flood frequency.

FEMA NRI riverine frequency should **not** be used as C. It is the mean number of Storm Events flood event-days over about 24 years starting around 1996, and tracts inherit the county value ([FEMA NRI](https://hazards.fema.gov/nri/annualized-frequency)). Its data source, period and reporting process therefore all overlap Y. The Global Flood Database (2000–2018) and Dartmouth Flood Observatory polygons also fail. They fall inside the study window, they map only large news-reported events, and GFD stops before most of the test period ([GEE Global Flood DB](https://developers.google.com/earth-engine/datasets/catalog/GLOBAL_FLOOD_DB_MODIS_EVENTS_V1); [DFO](https://floodobservatory.colorado.edu/Archives/index.html)).

Static physical susceptibility sits naturally beside C. The Fathom/First Street 30 m hazard maps find about **41M Americans in the 1% floodplain versus about 13M under FEMA maps** ([Wing et al. 2018](https://ioppublishing.org/news/usa-flood-risk-vastly-underestimated-study-shows/)). The combined fluvial–pluvial version reports a Critical Success Index of about 0.69–0.82 against benchmark maps ([Bates et al. 2021](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10263939-combined-modeling-us-fluvial-pluvial-coastal-flood-hazard-under-current-future-climates)). Two cautions follow. These maps were produced in 2018–2021 and include flood defences, so they partly embed A and post-2016 development. And Brody and Highfield report that the FEMA 100-year floodplain is a **poor predictor of where losses occur** ([TAMU](https://newsarchive.arch.tamu.edu/news/2013/5/29/floodplain-losspredictor/)). Cleaner pre-treatment susceptibility comes from HAND (height above nearest drainage) ([Nobre et al. 2016](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/hyp.10581)), slope, drainage density and soil hydrologic group. None of these has a published link to US county loss [gap].

## Exposure and adaptation can be measured, but lagged A carries flood history

Exposure is the strongest non-hazard lever in the SHELDUS literature. Liu, Hertel and Diffenbaugh ran a Tobit with county fixed effects on Indiana SHELDUS flood damage for 1995–2012. They found **87% of projected damage growth came from more houses in high-risk flood zones**, measured with FEMA maps and Census housing ([Stanford EMF](https://web.stanford.edu/group/emf-research/docs/PIAMDDI/Liu%20et%20al_Flooding_PI_2013.pdf)). Impervious surface raised county flood damage in Texas ([Brody et al. 2008](https://www.pubmed.gov/18217915/)), and wetland alteration raised it in Florida ([Brody et al. 2007](https://planning.org/knowledgebase/resource/9200805)). A widely quoted figure of roughly $4,000 of damage per m² of pavement comes from an interview, not a peer-reviewed estimate, and should not be cited ([HPB Magazine](https://www.hpbmagazine.org/?p=1996)). **Annual NLCD (1985–2023, 30 m)** is the one exposure series that varies every year across the whole window. It provides land cover and fractional impervious surface ([USGS Annual NLCD](https://www.usgs.gov/centers/eros/science/annual-national-land-cover-database?page=1)). The USACE National Structure Inventory gives structure values whose county aggregates are "reasonably accurate," but it is a 2022 snapshot ([GEE community catalog NSI](https://gee-community-catalog.org/projects/nsi/)).

Evidence on vulnerability for *property* loss is weaker. Tellman et al. studied 11,629 non-coastal US flood events from 2008–2012. **Individual census variables outperformed the composite SoVI**, and normalized damage was higher in rural, poorer, lower-house-value counties ([MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/12/15/6006)). Rufat et al. found that social-vulnerability indices **explained property loss poorly** after Hurricane Sandy ([Princeton](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/how-valid-are-social-vulnerability-models-3/)). CDC SVI also has vintage and field-name breaks across its 2000–2022 releases ([ATSDR](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/place-health/php/svi/svi-data-documentation-download.html)). The case therefore favours raw components (income, poverty, median value, mobile-home share, pre-1980 housing) over the index. No source isolated mobile-home or building-age effects on county property loss [gap].

Adaptation evidence is real but threshold-shaped. CRS communities had **about 50% lower insured losses per year**, with freeboard and open-space protection the most effective activities ([Highfield & Brody, IJDRR](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2212420916303983)). In Gulf data, however, claim reductions appeared **only at Class 5 or better** [snippet] ([Frimpong et al.](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/284989/files/Manuscript_Frimpong_19-1.pdf)). FEMA loss-avoidance studies credit Iowa buyouts with avoiding **$98.7M in the 2008 floods (BCR 2.19)** [secondary summary] ([AIR Worldwide](https://www.air-worldwide.com/blog/posts/2014/4/floodplain-acquisition-programs-can-reduce-future-losses/)). The DAG's C → A edge is empirically real. **NFIP take-up rises about 9–10% the year after a flood and decays over about 15 years** ([Gallagher 2014](https://www.montana.edu/gallagher/documents/flood_insurance_aeja.pdf)). Buyouts concentrate in richer, more populous counties after declarations ([Mach et al. 2019](https://www.science.org/doi/pdf/10.1126/sciadv.aax8995)). A therefore has to be measured as of the prior year. Even then, it partly encodes past floods. That is acceptable for prediction, but A's coefficient cannot be read causally. The sources for A are OpenFEMA FimaNfipPolicies (policies in force by county FIPS, derived from effective and termination dates) ([rfema](https://ropensci.r-universe.dev/rfema/doc/getting_started.html)), HMA Mitigated Properties joined to Projects for county ([data.gov](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/hazard-mitigation-assistance-mitigated-properties)), and semiannual CRS lists ([data.gov CRS](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/national-flood-insurance-program-nfip-community-rating-system-dataset-bd904)). **No machine-readable historical CRS panel for 2000–2023 was found**, and early-2000s policy coverage in OpenFEMA is unconfirmed.

Several variables are forbidden as inputs. NFIP claims, IA/PA obligations, same-month or later disaster declarations, repetitive-loss counts and post-event HMA grants are all loss-derived. SHELDUS also splits multi-county event totals evenly across counties [snippet] ([NY Fed SR1135](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr1135.pdf)). Any loss-derived feature would share that measurement error with Y.

## Hurdle models with spatial pooling fit a 97%-zero outcome

No paper models SHELDUS county-month flood loss with a hurdle or ML and reports ranking skill. The adjacent precedents are consistent with one another, though:

| Precedent | Design | Relevance |
|---|---|---|
| Liu et al., Indiana SHELDUS ([mygeohub](https://mygeohub.org/resources/1200/download/Liu-et_al-Climatic_Change.pdf)) | Tobit, county fixed effects, annual | Econometric baseline; assumes one latent process for occurrence and size |
| Delmarva SHELDUS crop loss, 2025 ([SERRA](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00477-025-03157-z)) | Classify-then-regress with RF/SVM vs GLMM vs **county × month average baseline** | Closest SHELDUS ML template; metrics not retrieved |
| Harris County flash flood ([NHR](https://doi.org/10.1061/nhrefo.nhreng-1729)) | Two-part mixed-effects hurdle | Direct precedent for a logistic + log-linear hurdle |
| CONUS NOAA flood events, 2026 ([Hydrology](https://doi.org/10.3390/hydrology13010031)) | RF best, GBR compared; 38 features cut to 18 | Feature pruning stabilized results [snippet] |
| Guangdong TC county loss ([IJDRR](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2212420924007179)) | **LightGBM best** of RF/XGB/LGBM/CatBoost | Supports gradient boosting [snippet] |
| Portugal wildfire, "XGBoost Meets INLA" ([Environmetrics 2026](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/env.70089)) | Hurdle + **BYM2** in R-INLA + XGBoost hybrid | Closest template for a hurdle with Bayesian spatial terms |
| French flood claims, 2026 ([arXiv 2607.07609](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.07609)) | GLM vs iCAR/BYM/SPDE in INLA | Spatial random fields on flood losses |

With about 97% zeros, a hurdle beats a Tobit because occurrence and severity plausibly have different drivers. The Spekkers result above is one reason. A Tweedie (compound Poisson–gamma) objective is a one-part alternative from insurance pricing. It is available in LightGBM, XGBoost and scikit-learn ([scikit-learn example](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/auto_examples/linear_model/plot_tweedie_regression_insurance_claims.html); [Yang, Qian & Zou](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1508.06378)). It is worth running as a comparison, although no county flood application was found.

The Bayesian spatial component earns its place through pooling. BYM2 writes the county effect as σ(√(1−ρ)·θ + √(ρ/s)·φ), where φ is ICAR and s is a graph-derived scaling factor. This makes ρ interpretable as the spatial share of variance. The parameterisation works only on connected graphs ([PyMC BYM notebook, opened](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pymc-devs/pymc-examples/main/examples/spatial/nyc_bym.myst.md)). The same notebook states the forecasting intuition directly: a low-loss county ringed by high-loss neighbours "probably just had good luck in the past." That intuition fits counties with only a handful of positive months in 2000–2016. BYM2 with PC priors is recommended over Leroux, although the two often differ trivially in fit ([arXiv 1802.06350](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1802.06350)). R-INLA's built-in zero-inflated families have a **scalar, intercept-only zero probability** and are meant for counts ([R-INLA doc, opened](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/grantbrown/inla/master/r-inla.org/doc/likelihood/zeroinflated.tex)). A dollar-loss hurdle with covariates in both parts therefore needs two models, or a joint binomial + Gaussian-on-log model with a shared (`copy`) spatial effect [mailing-list snippet] ([r-inla group](https://groups.google.com/g/r-inla-discussion-group/c/DPvHIGcbd0E)). PyMC's `pm.ICAR` works but MCMC on about 300k county-months is slow. In PyMC, the spatial part should be fitted on aggregated data. brms (`car(..., type="bym2")` with `hurdle_lognormal`) is a one-formula alternative [from general knowledge, unverified]. Moraga's Chapter 9 introduces BYM and the INLA call on a cross-sectional Poisson disease-mapping example ([Moraga Ch. 9](https://www.paulamoraga.com/book-spatial/bayesian-spatial-models.html)). The chapter was not opened, and the project must add the temporal and hurdle extensions itself.

Spatial confounding is an interpretation hazard, not mainly a prediction one. Adding a spatial random effect can shift or inflate the variance of coefficients on spatially smooth covariates such as precipitation climatology or impervious share. Restricted spatial regression gives overconfident intervals and is not a remedy ([Khan & Calder, PMC9540349](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9540349/)). Driver coefficients should therefore be reported with and without the BYM2 term.

On the causal side, the canonical design treats within-unit weather as quasi-random after unit and time fixed effects. Adaptation is then estimated as a W × C interaction ([Hsiang 2016](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22181)). Hsiang and Narita found **+20.3% damage per 1 m/s wind, falling to 19.6% per unit of higher climatological exposure** ([IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wsi/ccexxx/v03y2012i02ns201000781250011x.html)). Bakkensen and Mendelsohn argue adaptation effects are more than ten times larger ([IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ucp/jaerec/doi10.1086-685908.html)). The literature has not settled the size of adaptation. Kreibich et al. (45 paired events) found risk management lowers impacts for comparable events but **almost always fails when the second event is much more extreme** ([Lund](https://lup.lub.lu.se/record/a6a9b4e5-0910-4d1d-803f-0e353caea896)). So τ(w; x, c) should be allowed to cross, with adapted high-C counties doing better at moderate w but not at extreme w, rather than being forced monotone. DML and causal forests have been applied to flood *mitigation programmes* (CRS via CausalFlow, [arXiv 2312.03843](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.03843)), but **not to hazard intensity → county loss** [gap].

Evaluation practice follows from the data. At 3% prevalence, PR-AUC is more informative than ROC-AUC. Hazard-occurrence AUCs are often modest; one district study reports XGBoost at 0.675 ([JISI 2026](https://journal-isi.org/index.php/isi/article/download/1461/734)). Blocked rather than random cross-validation is required wherever dependence exists ([Roberts et al. 2017](https://www.biometrie.uni-freiburg.de/mitarbeiter/dormann/roberts-et-al-2017-ecography.pdf)). Insurance practice ranks risk with Lorenz/Gini curves, which correspond directly to "share of loss captured by the top-k counties" ([scikit-learn](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/auto_examples/linear_model/plot_tweedie_regression_insurance_claims.html)). No flood paper reports top-k or interval coverage for county loss, so the project's numbers will have no external benchmark.

## Recommended variable set and method plan

The following table is the concrete feature set. Every entry is non-SHELDUS and measured before the target month. Thresholds use pre-2000 baselines, or training-period data only.

|---|---|---|---|
| W | P_month; Rx1day, Rx3day, Rx5day; days above county p95/p99 | PRISM daily `OREGONSTATE/PRISM/ANd` (or nClimGrid-Daily) | Percentiles and GEV 2/10/25-yr levels from 1981–1999 |
| W | Exceedance flag: Rx5day > county 10-yr return level | same | Captures the "extreme wet tail" |
| W | Prior 1- and 2-month precipitation anomaly (SPI-style) | PRISM monthly `ANm`, baseline 1895–1999 | Antecedent wetness |
| W | Root-zone soil moisture (start of month) and its percentile | ERA5-Land daily, layers 1–3; baseline 1950–1999 | Avoid SMAP (starts 2015) |
| W | SWE at prior month end, ΔSWE / snowmelt; days with Tmin < 0 in prior month | ERA5-Land; PRISM tmin | Rain-on-snow and frozen-ground signal |
| W | Heavy-rain × wet-soil interaction | derived | Tests the Berghuijs mechanism |
| W (optional) | Monthly max discharge / gauge Q2, or stage − NWS flood stage | USGS NWIS | Needs gauge-to-county/HUC crosswalk; captures upstream mainstem floods |

| X | Developed % and mean impervious % (prior year) | Annual NLCD | Only fully annual exposure series |
| X | Housing units, median home value, population | NHGIS 2000/2010/2020 + ACS 5-yr, interpolated | Use as exposure offset / scale |
| A / vulnerability | Poverty %, median income, mobile-home %, % built pre-1980, rurality | NHGIS / ACS | Components, not SVI |
| A (lagged) | NFIP contracts in force per HU (prior year) | OpenFEMA FimaNfipPolicies | Check early-2000s completeness |
| A (lagged) | Best or HU-weighted CRS class; indicator for Class ≤ 5 | FEMA CRS lists | Assemble historical panel by hand |
| A (lagged) | Cumulative HMA acquisitions/elevations to prior year | OpenFEMA HMA Mitigated Properties + Projects | |
| A | Wetland share (prior year), 5-year wetland change; leveed-area share, dam count (static) | Annual NLCD classes 90/95; NLD; NID | Levee effect means sign may flip at extreme W |

The method plan has six stages, each building on the last. Stage one is the baselines: county × calendar-month climatology (occurrence rate and mean log-loss from 2000–2016) and the existing logistic + log-OLS hurdle. Duan smearing should be applied whenever log predictions are converted back to dollars. Stage two is the main predictive model: a LightGBM hurdle with a binary objective for occurrence and a log-normal (or Tweedie, as a one-part comparison) model for severity. It takes W, C, X and A as inputs, is tuned by rolling origin (train to 2012, validate 2013–2016), and its W block is ablated (monthly only, plus Rx, plus antecedent/snow) to fill the head-to-head gap the literature leaves open. Stage three is the Bayesian spatial model. The cheapest first step is a Poisson-BYM2 on county counts of damage months for 2000–2016, with offset log(204). Its posterior propensity becomes a feature for stage two and a stand-alone ranking. The full model is an R-INLA joint hurdle: a binomial occurrence part and a Gaussian-on-log severity part, each with covariates, a BYM2 county effect (optionally shared via `copy`), a cyclic month effect and an rw1 year effect. Test responses are set to NA to produce 2017–2023 forecasts. An iid-only county effect serves as the ablation, and the queen-contiguity graph must be checked for islands. Stage four is the causal reading of τ(w; x, c), under two designs. The first is county and year-month fixed effects with W × C interactions (Hsiang–Narita). The second is a causal forest or DML that conditions on C and static X, with W as a continuous treatment. Overlap of W across C bins should be checked before interpreting high-w effects in low-c counties, and crossing shapes (Kreibich) should be allowed.

Stage five is evaluation. Occurrence is scored with ROC-AUC, PR-AUC, Brier score and Brier skill against climatology, plus a reliability diagram. Ranking is scored on 2017–2023 county totals with Spearman ρ, top-50/100 hit rate, loss captured@k and Lorenz/Gini. Severity is scored with MAE on log-loss for positive months, and uncertainty with empirical 80/90/95% interval coverage (native in INLA, conformal for the tree models). All metrics should be reported by test year, because a year such as 2019 can dominate, with moving-block bootstrap intervals over years. Stage six is interpretation hygiene: report driver coefficients with and without the spatial term, given spatial confounding.

## Conclusion

The literature changes this project's centre of gravity. The obvious W variable, same-month county rainfall, is probably not the strongest Midwest predictor. Antecedent soil moisture, snowpack and frozen ground, plus upstream mainstem conditions, did the damage in the defining test-period event (2019). A model that omits them will look worst exactly where losses are largest. The DAG also has a practical edge. Because NFIP take-up and buyouts respond to past floods, lagged A is partly a proxy for C. That makes a clean pre-2000 climatological C essential, both to keep the C → A path honest and to give the W × C interaction in τ(w; x, c) any meaning.

Two gaps turn into contributions. No study has ranked hazard formulations, or tested a hurdle-BYM2 model, on SHELDUS county-month loss. So the W-block ablation and the spatial-versus-non-spatial comparison are results in their own right, not just diagnostics. Their value depends on reporting PR-AUC, loss captured@k and interval coverage by test year against a climatology baseline that may prove hard to beat.
